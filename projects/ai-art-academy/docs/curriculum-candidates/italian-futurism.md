## Why it belongs in the Academy

Italian Futurism (launched by F. T. Marinetti's 1909 manifesto) gives learners a
genuinely different lesson from every movement already in the curriculum: instead of
depicting a static subject in a new palette or line quality, it tries to depict
**motion, speed, and simultaneity** — a moving body, a running horse, or a city street
shown as if several instants of time were compressed into one image. Where Cubism
(§14) fractures a *static* subject into multiple viewpoints, Futurism fractures a
*moving* one into multiple instants — a related but distinct idea worth teaching
side-by-side with Cubism rather than folded into it.

## Recognition cues

- overlapping, semi-transparent repetitions of a single form suggesting successive
  instants of motion (a stepping leg, a running dog's legs, a speeding car)
- sharp diagonal "force lines" radiating from or trailing behind a moving subject
- fragmented, crystalline facets similar to Cubism, but organized around implied
  motion and energy rather than a fixed viewpoint
- subjects drawn from modern speed and industry: automobiles, trains, crowds,
  machinery, urban street scenes — a deliberate rejection of static, past-facing
  subject matter (museums, moonlight, ruins) that the founding manifesto singled out
- dynamic, often unstable compositions with no single resting point for the eye

## Historical frame

Marinetti's *Manifesto of Futurism* (published in Milan and in Paris's *Le Figaro*,
1909) called for art to embrace speed, machinery, youth, and violence, and explicitly
to abandon the veneration of the past. Painters Umberto Boccioni, Giacomo Balla,
Carlo Carrà, Gino Severini, and Luigi Russolo signed the 1910 *Manifesto of Futurist
Painters* and *Futurist Painting: Technical Manifesto*, translating Marinetti's
literary program into a visual one built on "dynamism" — the idea that a painting or
sculpture should convey an object's motion and force, not just its static appearance.

The movement is also historically fraught: several of its founders (Marinetti chief
among them) later aligned with Italian Fascism, and the manifesto's language
explicitly glorified war and violence. Teach this honestly as part of the movement's
history — Futurism is not neutral formal innovation, and the curriculum should not
present its embrace of speed and machinery without also noting what its founders
said that embrace was for.

## Public-domain and generation policy

For displayed examples: use only the three works above (or others independently
verified the same way against the Met's own `isPublicDomain` field), record
creator/title/date/collection/accession/rights statement, and never substitute a
newly generated "example" for one of these documented works.

For generation:
- use movement-level instructions (dynamism, force lines, fragmented repetition of
  a moving form) — never Boccioni's name in the generation prompt itself, matching
  every other single-anchor-artist entry's convention (Precisionism §33, Arts and
  Crafts §35);
- do not glorify violence, war, or speed-at-any-cost imagery — the manifesto's own
  language did, the Academy's teaching of it should not;
- do not reduce the movement to "generic Cubism with speed lines" — the specific
  device is *implied motion through repeated/overlapping form*, not simple faceting.

## Try It — one composition, contrast with Cubism

Start with a photo of a person or animal caught mid-motion (walking, running,
jumping).

1. Remix it as **Cubism** (`cubism`): fracture the subject from multiple *static*
   viewpoints at once, as if several cameras photographed it simultaneously from
   different angles.
2. Remix it as **Italian Futurism** (`italian-futurism`): fracture the subject into
   overlapping repetitions that suggest it moving *through time*, with force lines
   showing its trajectory.

Compare what each remix keeps and discards. A successful pair should make clearly
different arguments about the same moving subject — one about simultaneous
viewpoint, one about simultaneous instants.

## Reflect

- Does the result read as "an object moving" or just "an object with lines added"?
- Which parts of the composition carry the sense of speed, and which stay still?
- How does force-line placement change the implied direction and speed of motion?
- The founding manifesto called for rejecting the past and embracing speed,
  machinery, and (explicitly) violence — what does the movement's visual language
  gain from that stance, and what should a modern viewer be skeptical of?
- Would the image still read as Futurist if every force line were removed?

## Common generation failures

### Cubism with speed lines

Symptoms: a faceted, static-looking image with a few streaks added, no sense that
anything is actually moving through the frame.

Correction: specify overlapping *repetitions of the same form* at slightly
different positions, not just fractured facets — the repetition is what implies
motion, not the facets alone.

### Generic motion-blur filter

Symptoms: a photographic motion-blur or "zoom blur" effect standing in for the
movement's actual structural device.

Correction: request discrete, semi-transparent repeated silhouettes and diagonal
force lines rather than a continuous blur gradient — Futurist dynamism is
constructed, not photographic.

### Losing the subject

Symptoms: the fragmentation goes so far that the original subject is no longer
recognizable at all.

Correction: preserve enough of the subject's silhouette and identity in the
foremost/most-opaque repetition that a viewer can still tell what is moving.
