## Why this belongs in the Academy

Ancient Egyptian painting is arguably the single most historically
consequential two-dimensional visual system in the curriculum — it fixed a
set of pictorial conventions (composite/"twisted" perspective, hierarchical
scale, flat registers, integrated hieroglyphic text) that were followed with
remarkable consistency for well over two thousand years, and it is the
starting point of nearly every general art-history survey. Nothing already
in the curriculum teaches this visual grammar: Fayum Mummy Portraits (§28)
is a *later*, Greco-Roman-influenced, naturalistically modeled style painted
directly on mummy wrappings in Roman Egypt (A.D. 50-250), and is in most
ways the stylistic *opposite* of what this entry teaches — a useful contrast
the lesson prose calls out directly rather than leaving implicit (the same
kind of explicit cross-reference already used between `song-dynasty-landscape`
and `joseon-genre-painting`, or `persian-miniature` and `mughal-miniature`).

The style is also unusually well suited to remix teaching because its rules
are procedural rather than personal: no single named "genius" invented it,
it does not depend on brushwork idiosyncrasy, and its core devices
(composite perspective, hierarchical scale, register bands, flat unmodeled
color, integrated caption text) can be described and checked for as a
repeatable visual grammar — closer in spirit to how this curriculum already
teaches Byzantine Mosaic (§2) or Greek Vase Painting (§1) than to a
single-artist movement.

## Recognition cues

- **Composite ("twisted") perspective on the human figure:** head, legs, and
  feet shown in profile; eye and shoulders/torso shown frontally — the
  figure assembled from each body part's clearest angle rather than one
  consistent viewpoint.
- **Hierarchical scale:** the most important figure in a scene (a god, the
  pharaoh, or the tomb owner) drawn noticeably larger than servants,
  family members, or offering-bearers, regardless of actual physical
  distance or position.
- **Registers:** the picture divided into stacked horizontal ground-line
  bands, each holding its own self-contained scene or procession, read left
  to right (or right to left) and top to bottom.
- **Flat, unmodeled color fill inside firm outlines:** no cast shadow, no
  atmospheric perspective, no gradual tonal blending — color areas are
  bounded and even.
- **A documented color-coding convention:** men's skin is typically painted
  reddish-brown, women's skin paler yellow — a real, extensively documented
  period convention, worth naming factually as an art-historical fact about
  how gender was visually coded, not as a style choice to casually imitate
  on arbitrary modern subjects.
- **Integrated hieroglyphic text:** captions, names, and offering formulas
  are worked directly into the composition as part of its visual design,
  not added afterward as a separate label.
- **Deities identifiable by attribute:** animal heads (jackal-headed
  Anubis, falcon-headed Horus), specific crowns, and held symbols (ankh,
  was-scepter) mark divine figures within an otherwise human-scaled scene.
- **Common subjects:** offering tables piled with bread/beer/produce,
  funerary processions, agricultural and craft-workshop scenes, banquets,
  and — in funerary papyri specifically — the weighing-of-the-heart
  judgment scene before Osiris.

A useful diagnostic question: **does the image use one camera-like
viewpoint, or does every part of every figure show its own "best" angle at
once?** Egyptian painting is built entirely on the second answer.

## Historical frame

The conventions described above appear already essentially fully formed in
Old Kingdom tomb decoration (from roughly the 26th century B.C.) and were
followed, with real but bounded variation, through the New Kingdom and into
the Third Intermediate Period covered by this entry's verified examples
(c. 1390-950 B.C.) — a continuity of well over a thousand years by the time
of the works cited here, and closer to two and a half thousand years
counting the tradition's full run. The style was not primarily decorative:
it was believed to function magically, providing the deceased with a
permanent, working supply of food, servants, and protection in the
afterlife, which is part of why proportion, completeness, and correct
labeling mattered more than optical realism.

This lesson's three verified example works are funerary papyri from the
"Book of the Dead" tradition (a modern name for a genre ancient Egyptians
called something closer to "Book of Going Forth by Day") rather than tomb
*wall* paintings specifically. This is a deliberate, disclosed scoping
choice: papyrus vignettes use the exact same pictorial grammar described
above (they were often produced by the same workshops that decorated tomb
walls, and share every recognition cue listed) but survive today as
portable, well-documented museum objects with a much cleaner rights chain
than most in-situ tomb wall paintings — most surviving wall-painting
photography is either a copyrighted modern photograph of a fragile
in-country monument, or an early-20th-century facsimile copy whose own
copyist may not clear this policy's rules (several are attributed to
Metropolitan Museum Egyptian Expedition artists who died well within the
last 70 years — checked and deliberately not used here, see Verification
below). A future candidate could revisit tomb-wall facsimiles specifically
if a fully rights-clear copyist is identified; this entry does not attempt
that and scopes itself to papyrus examples instead.

## Artists

**Rights boundary:** No individual artist is named or identifiable anywhere
in this entry, and none may be added later without independently clearing
PUBLIC-DOMAIN-POLICY.md §1.3's both-prongs rule and being checked against
§4's living-artist exclusion. This is itself the strictest possible rights
boundary this project uses — there is no name to protect or misuse because
none is claimed.

**No individual artist is named or identifiable**, by design of this entry.
No papyrus or tomb painting in this tradition carries a signature in the
modern sense, and while some individual craftsmen from the workers' village
of Deir el-Medina are known by name from other documents (ostraca, tomb
inscriptions), none is reliably linked to any specific *surviving painted
work* used as an example here. This lesson credits the tradition to its
anonymous scribes and painters (`anonymous-egyptian-painters`), the same
treatment already used for Byzantine Mosaic's anonymous Ravenna and
Constantinople mosaicists (§2) and Fayum Mummy Portraits' anonymous
encaustic painters (§28). Because no individual is named, PUBLIC-DOMAIN-POLICY.md
§1.3's first prong (artist died 70+ years ago) carries the widest possible
margin — there is no living-memory risk whatsoever; the entire tradition's
active production ended well over two thousand years ago.

Checked and deliberately excluded: several Metropolitan Museum facsimile
copies of tomb wall paintings (produced 1907-1941 by the Museum's Egyptian
Expedition Graphic Section) are catalogued with a named modern copyist —
e.g. Norman de Garis Davies (1865-1941, clears the cutoff) but also Nina de
Garis Davies (1881-1965, an active copyist on the same project) and Charles
K. Wilkinson (1897-1986), both of whom die inside PUBLIC-DOMAIN-POLICY.md's
70-year window and are excluded even as prose per §4 rule 2. Rather than
build this entry around a mixed roster where some facsimile copyists clear
the cutoff and others don't (a genuinely confusable distinction for anyone
reading the lesson later), this entry scopes itself entirely to anonymous
original papyri, sidestepping the question altogether.

## Cultural and ethical framing

Ancient Egyptian antiquities carry a genuinely fraught modern collecting
history: most objects now held in Western museums (including all three
example works above) left Egypt during the 19th and early 20th centuries
under colonial-era excavation and "partage" (division-of-finds) systems
that gave foreign excavating institutions a share of what they found —
arrangements Egypt did not freely negotiate as an equal party, and that
modern Egypt has increasingly contested. The Academy should not present
"the Met has it" or "the API says public domain" as the end of the
conversation about these objects' history. The lesson's Reflect section
below asks learners to sit with this directly rather than treating a
correct copyright/public-domain determination as also settling questions
of cultural ownership, which it does not.

Separately: these are funerary objects made for specific named individuals'
burial and afterlife, not created as decorative art for public display.
Treat them with the same seriousness this project already applies to Fayum
Mummy Portraits (§28) — the deceased's name (Nauny, Tayuhenutmut) is not
incidental trivia. The Academy shows only the painted papyrus imagery
itself; it never displays, and generation guidance must never request,
imagery of actual human mummified remains.

## Public-domain and generation policy

For displayed examples: verify each work item-level against a holding
institution's own rights-status field before it enters the Academy — use
only the three works above (or others independently verified the same
way), record creator (here: "unknown," recorded as such — never a guessed
name), title, date, collection, accession/object ID, source URL, and
rights statement for each work individually, and never substitute a newly
generated "example" for one of these documented works.

For generation:
- do not include any artist names in generation prompts or presets, and
  exclude artist names from curriculum text and marketing copy — this
  entry has none to exclude today, but the rule stands for any future
  edit that tries to add one;
- use movement-level instructions describing the pictorial *system*
  (composite perspective, hierarchical scale, registers, flat color,
  integrated hieroglyphic-style caption marks) instead;
- avoid requesting imagery of mummies, sarcophagi contents, or human
  remains;
- avoid exoticizing the tradition with "ancient curse," "cursed tomb," or
  pulp-adventure treasure-hunting framing — teach it as a real, functioning
  visual and religious system, the same standard this project already
  holds itself to for Fayum Mummy Portraits and Byzantine Mosaic;
- avoid generic "sand dune desert" or "pyramid silhouette" filler imagery
  not requested by the source photo — the actual recognition cues are
  about figure construction and composition, not about pyramids.

## Movement-level remix configuration

```yaml
slug: egyptian-painting
mode: prompt
label: Ancient Egyptian Painting
instruction: >-
  Recompose this image in the manner of ancient Egyptian tomb and papyrus
  painting: redraw any human figures using composite perspective (profile
  head, legs, and feet; frontal eye and shoulders), organize the
  composition into one or more stacked horizontal register bands with a
  ground line, use flat unmodeled color fill inside firm dark outlines with
  no cast shadow, scale the most significant figure noticeably larger than
  secondary figures, and add small integrated hieroglyphic-style caption
  marks near key figures. Preserve the source subjects' identity and
  general arrangement. Avoid photorealistic shading, avoid pyramid or
  desert filler not present in the source, and avoid "cursed tomb"
  adventure-movie styling.
negative_guidance:
  - photorealistic shading or cast shadow
  - generic pyramid/desert filler imagery
  - "ancient curse" or pulp-adventure treasure framing
  - imagery of mummies or human remains
  - single consistent camera-like viewpoint on figures
preserve:
  - subject identity
  - general composition/arrangement
  - number and relative role of figures present
```

## Try It

### Exercise 1 — Build a figure the Egyptian way

Choose a photo with one standing or walking person. Remix it and compare:

- Which body parts read as profile, and which read as frontal?
- Does the figure still look coherent as "one person," even though no
  single camera angle could have produced it?

### Exercise 2 — Registers and hierarchy

Choose a photo with two or more people of different apparent importance
(e.g., one clearly the focal subject). Remix it and check:

- Did the model scale the central figure up relative to the others?
- If the source has a background/foreground, did the remix flatten it into
  distinct register-like bands?

### Exercise 3 — Contrast with Fayum

Run the *same* portrait photo through both `egyptian-painting` and
`fayum-mummy-portraits` (§28). Compare directly:

- Which one keeps a naturalistic, modeled face, and which one flattens it?
- Which one uses a single fixed viewpoint, and which one does not?
- Both are "ancient Egyptian" in a loose sense — what does this pair teach
  about why that label alone is not a useful style description?

## Reflect

- What does it mean that this style was built to work *functionally*
  (magically providing for the afterlife) rather than to look convincing
  to a living viewer?
- The men's-reddish/women's-pale skin-color convention is a real
  documented period practice. How should a lesson describe a real
  historical gender convention factually without recommending learners
  reproduce it on modern photos of real people?
- These specific objects left Egypt under colonial-era excavation
  agreements Egypt did not freely negotiate. Does confirming an object is
  legally "public domain" today settle any question about whether it
  should still be held outside Egypt? What is the difference between those
  two questions?
- These are funerary objects made for a specific named person's burial.
  How does knowing "Nauny" and "Tayuhenutmut" were real people change how
  you look at the imagery, compared to treating it as generic pattern
  source material?

## Common generation failures

### Generic "hieroglyphics wallpaper"

Symptoms: the model scatters decorative hieroglyph-like squiggles around
the border as ornament without actually restructuring the figures into
composite perspective or registers.

Correction: the recognition cues live in figure construction and
composition, not surface decoration — emphasize composite perspective and
register bands explicitly; treat caption marks as a small supporting
detail, not the whole style.

### Pulp treasure-hunt styling

Symptoms: torch-lit stone corridors, glowing curses, adventure-movie
lighting standing in for the actual painted-papyrus style.

Correction: explicitly negative-prompt "cursed tomb"/adventure framing;
anchor on flat painted color and register composition instead.

### Photorealistic face with a flat-color body

Symptoms: the model keeps naturalistic shaded skin on the face while only
flattening clothing or background — the opposite of the style's actual
uniform flatness.

Correction: explicitly request flat, unmodeled color on skin as well as
clothing, with no cast shadow anywhere in the figure.

### Single consistent viewpoint

Symptoms: the figure looks like an ordinary flattened cartoon from one
camera angle rather than composite perspective (profile legs with a
frontal torso).

Correction: describe the composite-perspective construction explicitly
(profile head/legs, frontal eye/shoulders) rather than relying on "ancient
Egyptian style" alone, which the model may resolve as a generic flat
cartoon look.
