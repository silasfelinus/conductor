# Precisionism — Curriculum Candidate

## Why it belongs in the Academy

Precisionism gives learners a clean bridge between Cubist simplification, American industrial modernity, photography, and later graphic design. It is especially useful for remix teaching because the style depends less on copying one painter's brushwork than on a repeatable visual grammar: hard-edged geometry, compressed depth, controlled light, and ordinary American structures treated as monumental forms.

The movement is taught here at movement level. The Academy should not offer imitation presets named after any living or recently deceased artist.

## Historical frame

Precisionism emerged in the United States during the 1910s and 1920s and remained influential through the 1930s. The label was applied retrospectively to artists who shared a highly controlled approach to factories, grain elevators, bridges, city architecture, machinery, and simplified rural buildings.

Its artists absorbed lessons from Cubism, Futurism, photography, commercial illustration, and machine-age design without forming a single manifesto-driven group. Their paintings often remove people, smoke, clutter, and visible brushwork from industrial scenes. The result can feel simultaneously celebratory, uncanny, and emotionally distant.

Useful historical questions include:

- When does an industrial landscape become a national symbol?
- What labor and environmental costs disappear when a factory is rendered as pristine geometry?
- Why did artists use European avant-garde techniques to construct a specifically American modern image?
- How does the absence of people change the politics of a workplace scene?

## Recognition cues

A Precisionist image commonly includes several of these traits:

- crisp, sharply bounded shapes
- simplified cylinders, rectangles, cones, and stepped planes
- compressed or shallow spatial depth
- clean architectural silhouettes
- little or no visible brush texture
- strong sunlight producing broad, exact shadows
- factories, silos, smokestacks, bridges, skyscrapers, barns, or machinery
- very few people, vehicles, signs, or incidental details
- a quiet, suspended atmosphere despite industrial subject matter
- compositions that feel designed rather than casually observed

## Public-domain artist context

### Charles Demuth (1883–1935)

Demuth transformed industrial buildings, signs, and vernacular architecture into layered geometric compositions. His work is useful for teaching how text fragments and architecture can become structural rather than merely descriptive.

Teaching caution: avoid reducing the movement to the visual grammar of *I Saw the Figure 5 in Gold*. Use it as context for number, sign, and architecture relationships—not as a template to reproduce.

### Charles Sheeler (1883–1965)

Sheeler worked in both painting and photography. His industrial and architectural images are valuable for comparing camera-derived framing with painted simplification.

Teaching caution: Sheeler died in 1965, so copyright status varies by work and jurisdiction. Use only source images whose public-domain or open-access status has been verified independently. Movement-level prompt language does not require copying a specific Sheeler work.

### Ralston Crawford (1906–1978)

Crawford extended hard-edged industrial geometry into increasingly abstract compositions.

Teaching caution: Crawford's works are generally too recent for a blanket public-domain assumption. Include him in historical prose only unless a museum or rights holder explicitly identifies a particular source as reusable.

### Georgia O'Keeffe (1887–1986)

Some early city and architectural paintings overlap with Precisionist concerns, but the Academy should not present O'Keeffe as a preset or use her name as a generation shortcut. Her work remains copyright-sensitive in many contexts and her broader practice cannot be flattened into this movement.

## Remix configuration

**Mode:** prompt-first movement treatment

**Core instruction:**

> Recompose the source as an early twentieth-century American Precisionist scene. Reduce architecture and machinery to crisp geometric masses, flatten incidental detail, use controlled daylight and exact-edged shadows, compress depth, remove visual clutter, preserve the source composition, and avoid copying any single artwork or artist signature.

**Optional emphasis terms:**

- hard-edged industrial geometry
- machine-age stillness
- simplified architectural planes
- broad exact shadows
- compressed photographic framing
- monumental ordinary structures
- clean cylindrical and rectangular forms

**Negative guidance:**

- no painterly impasto
- no neon cyberpunk lighting
- no futuristic machinery
- no busy crowds
- no distressed poster texture
- no photorealistic surface noise
- no imitation of a named artwork

## Composition-preservation test

Use one source image containing a building or large manufactured object. Generate two versions:

1. a faithful Precisionist treatment preserving the original camera position and major silhouettes
2. an over-abstracted version that allows geometry to replace too much of the source

Ask learners to identify the point where simplification stops clarifying the composition and starts discarding it.

## Try It

Choose a photograph of a parking garage, water tower, warehouse, bridge support, school building, grain silo, or household appliance.

Before generating, write down:

- the three largest shapes
- the dominant direction of light
- which details can disappear without changing the subject
- whether people should remain visible

Run the movement-level prompt, then compare the result to the notes. Revise only one variable at a time: shadow size, depth compression, geometric simplification, or human presence.

## Reflect

- Does the cleaned-up image make industry feel admirable, threatening, neutral, or unreal?
- What evidence of labor has been removed?
- Which environmental effects would exist outside the frame?
- Does geometric order reveal the structure more clearly, or sanitize it?
- How would the meaning change if workers, residents, or pollution were restored?

## Common generation failures

### Generic futuristic city

The model replaces historical industrial architecture with glossy science-fiction towers.

**Correction:** specify early twentieth-century American factories, silos, bridges, or vernacular buildings; prohibit futuristic machinery and neon lighting.

### Flat vector poster

The model interprets hard edges as contemporary vector art.

**Correction:** request painted tonal transitions inside the clean shapes, natural daylight, and subtle material differences without visible impasto.

### Cubism overwhelms the source

The model fractures every surface into unrelated facets.

**Correction:** ask for simplified whole masses, compressed depth, and stable silhouettes rather than analytical fragmentation.

### Industrial propaganda

The result becomes a heroic advertisement for technology.

**Correction:** request quiet observational distance, empty or ambiguous space, and neutral monumental framing rather than triumphalist symbols.

### Photorealism with stronger contrast

The image remains a photograph with sharpened shadows.

**Correction:** explicitly reduce texture, remove incidental details, simplify curved and rectangular forms, and compress depth.

## Source and licensing checklist

Before promoting this candidate into production curriculum:

- verify every displayed artwork through a museum, library, archive, or rights holder
- store title, creator, date, institution, source URL, and rights statement
- distinguish public-domain artwork from prose-only historical references
- do not infer reusable status from an artist's death date alone
- keep O'Keeffe, Crawford, and other copyright-sensitive artists out of generation preset names
- preserve prompt, model, seed, source-image, and any LoRA metadata for generated examples

## Suggested placement

Place Precisionism after Cubism and before American Scene / Regionalism. That order helps learners see how geometric abstraction became a language for American architecture and industry before later artists returned more visibly to people, regions, and social narrative.
