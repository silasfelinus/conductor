# Mountain Gate, Four Traditions

A controlled comparison lesson that tests how much a tradition foregrounds or omits
"background" entirely, using four courtly and scholarly painting traditions that never
overlap with any other set in this collection: Persian Miniature (Timurid/Safavid,
c. 1400-1600), Mughal Miniature (Imperial atelier, c. 1560-1650), Song Dynasty
Landscape (Northern Song, c. 950-1130), and Joseon Dynasty Korean Genre Painting
(c. 1750-1820). Every prior set (garden-gate, harbor, night-window, one-room,
everyday-modernity) draws its four movements from one c. 1870-1930 Western cluster;
flower-arranger widened that to Byzantine Mosaic, Ukiyo-e, Art Nouveau, and Vienna
Secession. This set goes further still: three of the four traditions here are entirely
outside the Western canon, and the fourth (Song) predates it by roughly seven centuries.

The specific comparison this set is built to teach: the same background element — a
distant mountain visible past a garden wall — is treated as flat ornament in one
tradition, as the dominant subject in another, as naturalistically receding space in a
third, and as functionally absent in a fourth. Learners should come away able to name
how much of the "same" scene each tradition actually keeps on the page.

## Fixed composition

Use one source image throughout:

- a traveler pauses at a garden gate, exchanging a coin for fruit held out by a seated
  fruit-seller
- a low garden wall stands behind the two figures; a distant mountain range is visible
  above the wall
- a small bird perches on the wall near the gate
- the fruit-seller's basket sits on the ground beside them, holding three or four
  pieces of fruit
- the camera is frontal, at standing eye level, framing both figures and the gate
  between them

Keep the two figures' identities, poses, and the coin/fruit exchange, the gate, the
wall, the bird, and the fruit basket unchanged across all four variants. What changes
between variants is how much of the mountain and wall survive at all, and how that
space is organized — that variability is the lesson, not an error to correct.

## Rights boundary

Teach movements and historically documented techniques, not imitation of a named
artist. Use only public-domain reference works from artists who satisfy the Academy
policy (`PUBLIC-DOMAIN-POLICY.md`). Do not place artist names in generation prompts,
negative prompts, presets, filenames, or promotional copy.

Persian and Mughal miniature painting are related but historically and politically
distinct courtly traditions (Timurid/Safavid Iran vs. Mughal India); Song Dynasty
landscape (China) and Joseon genre painting (Korea) are separate national traditions
five to eight centuries apart in the same general region. Do not collapse any of the
four into a single generic "Asian" or "Islamic" label in lesson copy, captions, or
comparison exercises — name each tradition, era, and geography explicitly every time,
matching the specificity already required for the Ukiyo-e entry in flower-arranger.
Present each as a courtly or scholarly practice with its own patrons, workshops, and
theoretical writing (Guo Xi's *Linquan Gaozhi* for Song landscape; the Herat and Tabriz
ateliers for Persian miniature; Akbar's *tarh/rang/chihra* workshop division for
Mughal miniature; the Dohwaseo court painting bureau for Joseon genre painting) rather
than as generic "exotic" ornament borrowed for visual variety.

Learners may upload an image they own or are authorized to transform. Before
promotion, confirm that every displayed example image is public domain or separately
licensed for reproduction and that its source and rights statement are recorded.

## 1. Persian Miniature

### Recognition cues

- no Western linear perspective: the distant mountain and any far figures placed
  higher on the page rather than drawn smaller
- flat, even lighting with no cast shadows
- brilliant, unmixed jewel colors (ultramarine, vermilion, gold leaf) in flat fields
  rather than blended
- dense surface ornament — patterned textiles, tiled or carved gate architecture,
  stylized foliage — rendered as precisely as the figures
- "cutaway" gate architecture showing structure and passage at once
- a framing border of fine floral or geometric pattern around the whole scene

### Remix prompt

> Preserve the exact garden-gate composition: the traveler and fruit-seller exchanging coin for fruit, the wall, the bird, and the fruit basket. Reinterpret the scene through movement-level Persian miniature ideas: the distant mountain placed higher on the page rather than smaller to suggest depth, flat even lighting with no cast shadows, brilliant unmixed jewel colors in flat fields, dense ornamental patterning on the gate and wall, a cutaway view of the gate structure, and a fine decorative border framing the whole page. Keep both figures, the coin-and-fruit exchange, the bird, and the basket legible.

### Negative guidance

- no named artists
- no Western atmospheric perspective or blended color
- no cast shadows
- no loss of either figure or the fruit exchange
- no illegible border pattern that obscures the scene itself

### Common failure

The model often treats "miniature painting" as a generic ornate-border filter over an
otherwise Western-perspective image. A successful result must actually reorganize
depth — the mountain higher, not smaller — and flatten lighting throughout, not just
add a decorative frame around an unchanged photographic scene.

## 2. Mughal Miniature

### Recognition cues

- individualized, naturalistic faces with fine stippled modeling, rounder and more
  volumetric than Persian miniature's flatter features
- the mountain recedes with atmospheric haze and diminishing scale — the opposite of
  Persian miniature's higher-not-smaller convention
- gold used locally and sparingly (jewelry, gate ornament, basket trim), not as a flat
  background field
- fine, dense brushwork rendering fabric and basketwork with near-scientific precision
- a wide decorative border of scrollwork or florals, distinct in style from the
  painting itself

### Remix prompt

> Preserve the exact garden-gate composition: the traveler and fruit-seller exchanging coin for fruit, the wall, the bird, and the fruit basket. Reinterpret the scene through movement-level Mughal miniature ideas: individualized, naturalistically modeled faces for both figures, the distant mountain receding through atmospheric haze and diminishing scale, gold used only locally on jewelry, gate ornament, or basket trim, finely detailed brushwork on fabric and basketwork, and a wide decorative floral border distinct from the scene inside it. Keep both figures, the coin-and-fruit exchange, the bird, and the basket legible.

### Negative guidance

- no named artists
- no flat gold background field (that is the Persian variant's convention, not this one)
- no loss of atmospheric recession in the mountain
- no missing basket, coin, or bird
- no crowded battle or court-procession elements not present in the source scene

### Common failure

The model may blur Mughal and Persian conventions together — flat gold background
plus naturalistic faces. A successful result needs the specific Mughal contrast:
naturalistic, receding landscape space combined with sparing, localized gold, not a
flat jewel-toned field behind modeled figures.

## 3. Song Dynasty Landscape

### Recognition cues

- monochrome or near-monochrome ink, with at most light desaturated color washes
- a single dominant, towering mountain organizing the whole composition
- both figures rendered tiny, dwarfed by the landscape's scale — no longer the
  compositional focus
- layered atmospheric space: mist or void separating near, middle, and distant ground
- dense, repeated textured brushstrokes ("cun" strokes) building the mountain's rock
  surface, not outline-and-flat-fill
- no cast shadows, no single fixed light source
- tall vertical hanging-scroll format emphasizing upward scale

### Remix prompt

> Reinterpret the garden-gate scene through movement-level Northern Song monumental-landscape ideas: a single towering ink mountain fills most of a tall vertical composition, built from dense repeated cun-texture brushstrokes rather than outline and flat color, with layered mist separating near, middle, and far ground. Shrink the traveler, the fruit-seller, the gate, the wall, and the bird to small, barely legible details near the base of the mountain — present but clearly subordinate to the landscape's scale. Use monochrome ink with at most a light desaturated wash; no cast shadow.

### Negative guidance

- no named artists
- no color beyond a light desaturated wash
- no Western perspective grid or vanishing point
- no removal of the figures entirely — they must remain present, if tiny
- no modern mountaineering or unrelated landscape elements not implied by the source scene

### Common failure

The model may render a generic "ink wash" filter over the original composition at its
original scale, keeping the figures large and central. A successful result must
actually re-stage the whole image: the mountain becomes the subject, the human
narrative becomes a small legible detail within it, not the frame's main content.

## 4. Joseon Dynasty Korean Genre Painting

### Recognition cues

- thin, quick, calligraphic brush lines describing both figures with very few strokes
- light, translucent color washes (pale ochre, dull red, indigo, soft gray) over ink
  outline, never opaque or richly layered paint
- little or no background: the mountain and wall reduced to at most a single
  suggested line, or omitted, with figures placed on bare paper
- a candid, everyday, faintly humorous mood — an ordinary market haggle, not a
  ceremonial exchange
- figures grouped in a loose, informal cluster rather than a formal frontal arrangement
- a small red seal (dojang) stamped in one corner
- no cast shadow, no linear perspective

### Remix prompt

> Reinterpret the garden-gate scene through movement-level late-Joseon genre-painting ideas: the traveler and fruit-seller rendered with a few confident, quick calligraphic brush lines and light translucent color washes over ink outline. Reduce the mountain and garden wall to at most a single suggested line, or drop them entirely, so the figures sit on largely bare paper. Give the coin-and-fruit exchange a candid, faintly humorous, everyday market mood rather than a formal one. Keep the bird and the fruit basket as small, economically drawn details. Add a small red seal stamp in one corner.

### Negative guidance

- no named artists
- no dense background, ornament, or gold (that belongs to the Persian/Mughal variants, not this one)
- no opaque, heavily layered paint
- no ceremonial or courtly staging — this tradition depicts ordinary people
- no loss of the bird or fruit basket as small legible details
- no invented calligraphic text beyond the seal mark

### Common failure

The model may default to generic "ink brush" or anime-adjacent line art without the
specific near-absence of background. A successful result must be sparse — bare paper
carrying most of the composition — not merely thinner brushwork over an otherwise full
scene.

## Blind comparison exercise

Hide the tradition labels and ask learners to rank each result on:

1. composition preservation (both figures, the coin-and-fruit exchange, the bird, the basket)
2. how much of the "background" (mountain, wall) survives, and how it is organized
3. tradition recognition
4. flatness vs. depth treatment
5. color approach (jewel-toned flat fields vs. atmospheric wash vs. monochrome ink vs. light translucent wash)
6. absence of generic "art filter" behavior

Then ask learners to identify the tradition, era, and geography of origin, and cite
three visible cues, before revealing the labels.

## Reflection prompts

- Rank the four results by how much of the mountain view survives at all — which
  tradition keeps it, which shrinks the figures to fit inside it, and which drops it
  almost entirely?
- Persian and Mughal miniature share flat-vs-atmospheric space as their defining
  contrast — where exactly does that difference show up in each result?
- Song Dynasty landscape and Joseon genre painting both use ink and restraint, but
  arrive at nearly opposite compositions (mountain-dominant vs. figure-on-bare-paper).
  What does each tradition consider worth depicting in detail?
- Which result is recognizable without relying on a named artist's signature
  mannerisms?
- What does comparing four traditions that predate photography by five to ten
  centuries teach about "background" as a choice, not a given?

## Promotion gate

Promote a set only when:

- the four results preserve the fixed composition closely enough for a fair comparison
  (both figures, the exchange, the bird, the basket — even where a tradition
  deliberately shrinks or omits the background)
- tradition recognition does not depend on protected artist names
- source and output rights are documented
- representation review finds no stereotyping, dehumanization, exoticizing framing, or
  collapsing of any tradition into a generic "Asian" or "Islamic" label — each of the
  four is named with its specific era, court or workshop, and geography
- prompts and metadata are sufficient to reproduce or audit the exercise
- weak results and negative findings remain documented rather than quietly replaced
