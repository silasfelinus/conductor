# Slow Looking Practice

A reusable observation routine for every AI Art Academy movement lesson.

## Purpose

Slow looking teaches students to notice before they label. The goal is not to guess the movement as quickly as possible. The goal is to build a visible chain of evidence: what is present, how it is organized, what effect it creates, and which historical ideas best explain those choices.

This routine works with Academy example works, public-domain starter images, and student remix results. It avoids requiring prior art-history vocabulary, so beginners can participate before they know names such as chiaroscuro, tesserae, or divisionism.

## The five-minute routine

### 1. Inventory — 60 seconds

Describe only what can be pointed to in the image.

- What shapes, figures, objects, or marks are present?
- Which colors occupy the most space?
- Where are the brightest and darkest areas?
- What kinds of lines or edges repeat?

Avoid interpretation during this pass. Replace “the person is frightened” with evidence such as “the eyes are wide, the mouth is open, and the diagonal lines lean toward the figure.”

### 2. Structure — 60 seconds

Trace how the image directs attention.

- Where does your eye land first?
- What pulls it there: scale, contrast, color, detail, direction, or placement?
- Does the composition feel balanced, crowded, flat, deep, still, or unstable?
- What changes when you cover the focal area with your hand?

### 3. Material clues — 60 seconds

Look for evidence of how the image was made or simulated.

- Are brushstrokes, dots, carved lines, tiles, paper texture, or smooth glazes visible?
- Do surfaces feel transparent, opaque, layered, scratched, printed, or assembled?
- Which details seem controlled and which seem spontaneous?
- In a generated remix, which material clues feel convincing and which feel cosmetic?

### 4. Meaning — 60 seconds

Move from observation to interpretation.

- What mood or idea does the structure create?
- Which visible choices support that reading?
- What might a viewer from the movement’s original period have noticed differently?
- What remains ambiguous?

Students should be able to attach every interpretation to at least one visible clue. Multiple readings are welcome when the evidence supports them.

### 5. Movement hypothesis — 60 seconds

Connect the evidence to the lesson’s recognition cues.

- Which two cues are strongest?
- Which expected cue is missing?
- Does the image fit the movement broadly, or only imitate one famous artist or decorative surface?
- What other movement could plausibly explain the same evidence?

The final answer should be phrased as a hypothesis, not a verdict: “I think this is closest to Pointillism because the surface is built from separate color marks and the light is optically mixed.”

## Before-and-after remix comparison

Use the same routine on the source and the remix, then compare them across four dimensions.

| Dimension | Source question | Remix question |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | What must remain recognizable? | What survived, disappeared, or changed identity? |
| Composition | How are attention and space organized? | Did the remix preserve the visual hierarchy? |
| Material | What medium or surface is implied? | Does the new material logic affect the whole image or sit on top like a filter? |
| Movement cues | Which cues are already present? | Which taught cues became clearer, weaker, or contradictory? |

A successful remix does not need to preserve every detail. It should preserve enough subject and compositional structure to make the historical transformation legible.

## Reflection prompts

Choose one after a lesson or remix attempt.

- Name one detail you missed during your first ten seconds with the image.
- Which recognition cue changed your interpretation most?
- What does this movement exaggerate, simplify, or refuse?
- Which part of the remix looks historically grounded, and which part looks like generic “art style” decoration?
- What single prompt change would better preserve the source composition?
- Compare two movements that share a cue. What evidence separates them?

## Facilitation notes

- Accept plain-language observations before introducing specialist terms.
- Ask “What makes you say that?” more often than “Is that correct?”
- Separate factual correction from interpretation. A date can be wrong; a mood reading can be supported or unsupported.
- Do not reward speed. Give students silent looking time before discussion.
- When generated output fails, treat the failure as evidence about the model and prompt, not as a student failure.
- Keep public-domain provenance visible when discussing example works so historical looking and ethical sourcing remain connected.

## Accessibility adaptations

- Provide the recognition cues as text rather than relying on color or visual memory alone.
- Allow zoomed crops and high-contrast views for detail inspection.
- Invite descriptions of texture, rhythm, weight, and spatial organization when color distinctions are difficult.
- Read prompts aloud and permit spoken, typed, or selected responses.
- Avoid timed scoring; the minute marks are facilitation guides, not performance limits.

## Suggested lesson placement

Use the five-minute routine after the lesson’s recognition cues and before “Try It.” Use the comparison table after the first remix, then end with one reflection prompt. This creates a consistent sequence across all movements:

1. Learn the historical context.
2. Observe an example work closely.
3. Form a movement hypothesis from evidence.
4. Remix a source image.
5. Critique the transformation.
6. Reflect and revise.
