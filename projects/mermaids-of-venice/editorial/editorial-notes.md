# Editorial Notes — Mermaids of Venice (Second Edition, 2012)

Structural and scene-level notes from a full read: pacing, clarity, continuity,
factual slips, and recurring tics. **Described, never rewritten** — the fixes are
yours to make. Pure typos live in `VERY-IMPORTANT.md`; representation concerns in
`cultural-awareness.md`.

Page numbers are printed book pages. Where a note likely overlaps work you're already
doing for edition 3, it's marked **[possibly already in revision]** — the diff shows
edition 3 reworks nearly every page, so treat these as "confirm, don't re-flag."

---

## The big structural patterns (whole-book)

**1. Lore and backstory land at the wrong moments.** The single most consistent
structural note across all 13 sections: exposition arrives in large blocks exactly
when momentum should tighten.
- The Tzaddikim catalog (~30 items, p.79) front-loads a chapter before anything happens.
- Vitoria König's decade-long origin story (pp.221–223) is dropped in as the rally is
  about to ignite.
- The Jerome Fitch biography (pp.258–259) interrupts a tense standoff for a character
  who never returns; Billy Chen's comic biography (p.269) follows immediately — two
  same-shaped digressions back to back.
- The cosmology info-dump (ch.58, pp.302–305) lands as a lecture at the emotional peak.
Consider staggering this material into the scenes rather than pausing for it.

**2. The vocabulary/definition device is overused.** The rare-word chapter titles,
the epigraph couplets, and the inline "the word for X is Y" gag (drapetomania p.49,
physiognomy p.51, mamihlapinatapai p.252, the "verbivore duel" pp.245–246, the
word-definitions throughout the finale) are charming individually and thematically
justified at the end (Bernard chooses words as his divine domain). But the *density*
makes it read as showing off by mid-book. Thinning would strengthen the payoff.

**3. Tonal whiplash.** Cosmic sex, a dead mother, and cartoon fleas collide within a
few pages (pp.27–29); comic serial suicide attempts hard-cut to a real, bleak death
(chunk 5); an explicit threesome cuts to a cosmology lecture to a disaster-escape
(chunk 12). The ambition is real and sometimes it's genuine counterpoint — but often
it currently reads as whiplash rather than design. Worth deciding, scene by scene,
whether the collision is doing work.

**4. Title vs. setting.** *Mermaids of Venice* is set overwhelmingly in Amsterdam;
Venice is the sunken offstage image and the literal mermaids appear only on p.313.
Presumably deliberate, but confirm the payoff lands — readers repeatedly noted
waiting for the title's promise.

**5. Verbal tics to sweep on a polish pass.** "déjà vu" (×3 in one chunk), "Trust me"
(Bernard), "brother" (Hermes/the King), "love"/"sugarlips" (Maya), "double take,"
"No problem," and the "Thank you / You're welcome / I love you" call-and-response
(used in the afterlife chapter and again in the closing train chapter). Effective
once; the close spacing blunts them.

## Continuity items to reconcile

- **Vida's timeline / age.** Chs.5–6 run her to ~15–16 (the "recall notice"), then
  ch.9 opens "At fourteen years old…" and reaches a "sixteenth birthday" — the jump
  back reads as an unsignposted reset. Separately, ch.15–18: the wedding is followed
  by "Six months later" (p.100/101) but her internal note says "Five months ago"
  (p.102). **[possibly already in revision]**
- **The cats' speech rule.** "The cats weren't speaking to her anymore" is stated
  firmly (early ch.2), yet the unnamed calico speaks on p.17. Likely an intended
  special-cat beat, but the firm rule makes it read as an inconsistency — signpost it.
- **Motel vs. hotel** within a single scene (pp.47–48): "motel bathtub," then "hotel
  dresser," then "her hotel room." Standardize per location.
- **The injured foot.** Vida's hurt foot is her "right foot" (p.197) but Estoban
  lifts his "left foot" mirroring her (p.199). If he's mocking/mirroring, fine; as
  written it's ambiguous.
- **Suitcase count.** Bernard returns "dragging three large suitcases" (p.218) but the
  show is later "packed in two suitcases" (p.227) and only two appear in the riot
  (p.233). The third is unaccounted for.
- **"Sunday" vs the Saturday rally.** Bernard's note promises "all will become clear
  on Sunday" (p.219) but the show pays off at the established Saturday rally. Day named
  appears to be a slip.
- **Decibels.** The tower is "licensed for one hundred and seventy" (p.227) but
  Anatoly's voice later broadcasts "at two hundred decibels" (p.231). Reconcile if the
  joke leans on the licensed max.
- **The wheelbarrow's anatomy.** The same object Vida hides in is a "lid," a "brim,"
  and a "belly" (pp.128–137) — a wheelbarrow has no lid; the shifting nouns muddy the
  physical picture.
- **The framing narrator.** The novel opens in the unborn child's first-person "I,"
  but the ending relocates the "I" to Bernard, whose closing letter is signed
  "-Bernard" (p.327). Satisfying as a reveal, but it slightly reassigns whose voice
  we've been hearing the whole time. **Worth a deliberate check of ch.1 against ch.66
  to confirm this reads as intended reveal rather than a seam.**

## Scene- and craft-level notes

- **The opening (pp.9–16)** is strong and distinctive, but the framing narrator then
  vanishes for chapters, and the "beauty as curse" premise is delivered as a static,
  told catalogue of Betty Lou's body, which works against the theme. The nude-capture
  scene pivots abruptly from her terror to the officers' rapture (see
  `cultural-awareness.md`).
- **The medical/hospital montage (pp.52–53)** and the "poked, prodded, nicked" list
  run long and dilute the genuinely strong fragmented-diagnosis payoff.
- **The Bernard/Vida training negotiation (pp.163–166)** is circular and overlong —
  several beats restate the same standoff. The keepers are the Top-Five list and the
  "You don't need me. I need you." reversal.
- **The "dancing is physics" analysis (pp.248–252)** runs ~3 pages of interior
  mechanics before the emotional payoff and delays the kiss. Good characterization,
  draggy placement.
- **Vitoria König** is vivid but nearly tips into cartoon villainy in her phone-rant
  (one-note threats/crudity); George's note (the "selling fishing boats to nomads"
  parable, p.190) is the humanizing core and would carry more if she were dimensioned
  earlier. Her quiet beat — crawling out to rest her head on her sleeping husband's
  lap (p.267) — is more devastating than any of the shouting; a model for her.
- **The action climax (chunk 13)** is aftermath-only: the tower falls but the force
  that fells it (the "quitting" pylons, ch.55) is offstage in the finale, so the
  disaster can feel like weather rather than plot. The rescue beats themselves land.
- **Reverse-time sequence (pp.125–126)** is inventive but demanding; the flash-cuts
  are momentarily hard to track (the underlying continuity is sound).
- **Fire/fight choreography** twice needs a clarity check: the Kaspar kerosene-and-
  separate-torch ignition (pp.126–128) and Vitoria's stair-fall "through me" blocking
  (p.237) each took a second read.
- **The talking-hat routine** and the self-animating bowler motif are fun and clearly
  foreshadowing, but recur often enough to risk diluting the payoff — confirm the
  beats escalate rather than repeat.

## Factual checks

- **Holds up:** "9409… a perfect square" (97², p.302); "Pompeii almost two-thousand
  years ago" (79 AD fits); the Ephraimite/Shibboleth linguistics (p.239); Zorya as
  Slavic dawn goddesses (reinvented as a triad — an author liberty, fine as fantasy).
- **Soft:** sand mandalas sited generically "In Nepal" (p.172) — they're a Tibetan
  Vajrayana tradition. "A cave outside Nepal" (p.104) — Nepal is a country; a city was
  likely intended. "Kaartjies" (p.325) is Afrikaans (South Africa), spoken by a
  *Kenyan* porter. Tonenili (p.310) is properly a Diné rain deity, not a trickster —
  though the text reframes all names as "one aspect with many names," so the
  syncretism reads intentional (representation implications noted in
  `cultural-awareness.md`).
- **Real people, real events:** the Fatty Arbuckle / Virginia Rappe aside (pp.149–150)
  editorializes a real contested sexual-assault-and-death case ("history exonerated
  Fatty") and names the dead woman only in passing — a factual/sensitivity call, not
  just a craft one.
