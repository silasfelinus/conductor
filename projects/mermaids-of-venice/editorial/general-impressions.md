# General Impressions — Mermaids of Venice (Second Edition, 2012)

Honest reader-level reactions from a full read. Advisory only — no prose was
rewritten, and nothing here is a to-do list. It's one careful reader's account of
what the book *is* and how it lands.

## The one-paragraph version

This is a genuinely original, ambitious, funny, and often beautiful novel that is
also overstuffed and unruly — a "dazzling, occasionally vulgar jewelry box," as one
of the guest readers put it. Its premise (dying, reincarnating gods scraping by as
street performers while Venice sinks) is fresh; its two leads (Bernard, the
invisible, terminally-ill over-thinker; Vida, the mixed Irish-Brazilian fire-dancer
grieving her dead wife) are vivid and easy to root for; and its best set-pieces are
memorable. What holds it back from its own best self is a recurring tension between
the tenderness at its core and a comic register that keeps reaching for shock,
crudeness, or vocabulary-flexing — plus a structure that front-loads lore and
digresses at the worst moments.

## What genuinely works

- **The voice and the imagination.** The dying-god-of-theatre Arlechino reduced to a
  flea circus; the House of Talking Cats with their four-day calendar and absurd
  honorifics; the semi-sentient Schrödinger rowboat; the god of reality television
  feeding on fifteen seconds of human desperation — these are the inventions of a
  real writer having real fun. The mythic register is "lush without being soupy."
- **Bernard.** His eidetic-pain interiority, his word-hoarding, his gallows humor
  through a string of failed suicide attempts, and finally his choice to become the
  patron god of street performers — he carries the book. Readers cared about him.
- **The Vida/Maya love story.** Across every section, the queer relationship at the
  book's heart is the thing readers single out as handled with the most care: grief,
  humor, real interiority, equal agency. It's the emotional anchor and the strongest
  argument for the whole novel.
- **The ending.** The "three souls" thread and the unborn-narrator frame both close
  cleanly, and Bernard's closing letter to his unborn child ("No one ever told me my
  story when I was alive… I thought it was only fair that you should get to know
  yours") is a warm, earned landing that reframes the entire book.
- **Individual chapters** that are dramatically alive: the mango/Eden scene, the
  Vitoria König standoff, the Papi/reality-TV-god confrontation, the near-death river
  vision.

## What holds it back

- **The comedy fights the tenderness.** The book earns real feeling and then
  repeatedly undercuts it with a crude aside, a shock gag, or a punchline at a
  marginalized character's expense (details in `cultural-awareness.md`). Readers
  noticed the whiplash and some felt their goodwill spent.
- **Vocabulary as a tic.** The rare-word chapter titles and inline definitions
  ("the word for X is Y") delight the first several times and start to read as
  showing off by the middle. Even admiring readers flagged it. It pays off
  thematically at the end (Bernard choosing words), so the instinct is sound — it's
  the *density* that wears.
- **Structure and pacing.** Lore and character backstory tend to land in big blocks
  at the moments tension should tighten — the Tzaddikim list, Vitoria's decade-long
  origin dropped mid-rally, the Jerome/Billy comic biographies stacked back-to-back,
  the cosmology info-dump at the climax. The material is good; the *placement*
  interrupts.
- **The title vs. the setting.** The book is called *Mermaids of Venice*, but the
  bulk is emphatically Amsterdam; Venice is a sunken offstage image and the literal
  mermaids appear only in the final pages. It works as a grace note, but readers kept
  waiting for the title's promise.
- **The action climax is thinner than the emotional one.** The tower falls with the
  antagonistic force (the quitting pylons) largely offstage, so the disaster can feel
  like weather rather than plot, even as the emotional climax (Bernard's choice,
  Vida's rescue) lands hard.

## The tonal signature, stated plainly

The book runs cosmic sex, dead mothers, cartoon fleas, dick jokes, genuine grief,
and theological cosmology — sometimes within a few pages. At its best this is
thrilling counterpoint; at its worst it's whiplash. A reader's enjoyment tracks
closely with how much they trust the collisions to be intentional. When the book
slows down and lets a scene *be* (the mango scene, Maya's ghost, the König standoff),
it's at its most powerful. It is least effective when it's performing cleverness or
reaching for a shock laugh.

## Bottom line

A book worth being proud of and worth continuing to fight with. The bones — premise,
Bernard, the Vida/Maya love, the ending — are strong enough to carry a reader through
the excess. The path from "good" to "the book you wanted it to be" runs through
restraint: trusting the tenderness, thinning the vocabulary flex, moving the lore
out of the tense moments, and re-examining the handful of comic beats that punch
down (see `cultural-awareness.md`). Guest-reader reviews — a delighted cat, a fair-
minded SF fan, and a moved-but-critical academic — are collected in `guest-reviews.md`;
structural and scene-level notes in `editorial-notes.md`.
