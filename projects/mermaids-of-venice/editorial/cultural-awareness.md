# Cultural Awareness — Mermaids of Venice (Second Edition, 2012)

Silas asked specifically for a careful pass on gaps in gaze and privilege. This is
that pass: specific, page-referenced, descriptive. It is **not** a verdict on the
book or on you, and it does not tell you what to change — you make every call. It's
one close reading of where a discerning reader (and three guest readers, see
`guest-reviews.md`) noticed the text using a person as an instrument, reaching for a
stereotype, or lingering in a gaze the scene didn't seem to intend.

A fairness note up front, stated because it's true and because it matters for reading
everything below: **the book's central queer relationship (Vida & Maya) is handled
with real care** — interiority, humor, grief, equal agency — and it recurs as the
emotional anchor across every section. The concerns here sit alongside that
achievement, not in place of it. Where the same book gets representation right and
then careless, the carelessness stands out *because* the care is real.

Severity labels (low / moderate / high) are one reader's calibration of how much a
given beat is likely to cost you with an attentive reader — not a moral score.

---

## 1. The character you already named: Hermes / Eshu (pp. 66–69) — HIGH PRIORITY

This is the one you flagged, and an independent reader with no knowledge of your
concern arrived at exactly the same place, unprompted. So it's not in your head.

What's on the page: the one prominent Black character is "an older black man" with a
"salt-and-pepper beard" and "crooked yellow teeth that could have bricked the road to
Oz," who calls everyone "brother," runs a pickpocket-magician hustle, stages a fake
death, hands Bernard the plot-coupon that launches the entire novel, murmurs "tell
Death Eshu says hello," and vanishes — one appearance, the name "Eshu" dropped
exactly once.

The precise mechanism, as the academic guest reader put it: *"Everyone in this book
is secretly a god deployed as a device; the instrumentality is structural. But Eshu
is the only one whose device comes pre-loaded with gold teeth, a con, and jive
cadence. Dignifying a Yoruba trickster and then coding him in minstrel shorthand,
then vanishing him — that's the Magical Negro with a mythology footnote."*

Two things make it sharper than a generic instance:
- The **"bricked the road to Oz" teeth line** is the specific image readers snag on —
  it's the minstrel-adjacent detail doing the damage, more than the role itself.
- The nearby **"Two Wongs Make Rice"** restaurant gag (p.60) sits close enough that a
  careful reader reads them together and concludes, in the academic's words, that "the
  racial ear here is not careful." The two beats reinforce each other.

Your three candidate directions (new researched origin / an iteration of Arlechino /
keep him and dialogue the trope on the page) all remain live — this pass doesn't pick
for you. But two observations that may inform the choice:
- The **structural** problem (appears once, purely to deliver a device, then gone) and
  the **surface** problem (gold teeth, jive cadence, "brother" patter) are separable.
  A reader flagged the surface coding as the sharper liability. Whichever origin you
  choose, the surface portrait is where the minstrel echo lives.
- The **"iteration of Arlechino" option** is interesting structurally: Arlechino is
  already the book's most fully-realized god (the flea-circus passage is some of your
  best writing), so folding this figure into that mythology would trade a one-scene
  cameo for a character with existing interiority — which is precisely the thing the
  "device with no inner life" critique is about.

## 2. Sexualized minors — the beats to look at regardless of edition (HIGH)

These are flagged apart from gaze because they're the ones most likely to stop a
reader cold or cost a recommendation, and they read as the villain/comedy doing it
rather than you — but the text stages them without counterweight.

- **p.114 (Adelmo the tram driver):** he fixates on a "teenaged girl in white shorts"
  — "Adelmo imagined sliding his owns hands into her pockets, then into her shorts…
  pulling her shorts down to her ankles." The fantasy is the comic *mechanism* (he
  hits Bernard because he's ogling her). Reconsidering the "teenaged" tag at minimum
  would defuse most of it.
- **pp.228–231 (Anatoly):** his act turns from a mother to her "young girl of about
  ten": "Call me, cutie. In three years." Staged as entertainment; the crowd "laughed
  nervously but didn't look away." A sexualized joke aimed at a named ten-year-old.
- **p.43 & p.49 (young Vida):** ~14-year-old Vida is appraised as the "tall and
  attractive woman she would become" with "olive-coffee skin," and at 14–16 enters a
  sexual "partnership" with the adult itinerant Tony T, who also introduces her to
  drugs — narrated as empowering apprenticeship, with the age/power gap never
  registered.

None of these require the reader to be looking for trouble; they surface on a normal
read.

## 3. The male gaze — the pattern you asked about

You named "moments of male gaze," and the honest finding is: **it's a reflex, not
just a moment.** The narrator's default when a female body appears is an eroticized,
part-by-part catalogue.
- The **Betty Lou nude-capture (pp.15–16)** is the clearest single case: the scene
  *intends* a "beauty as unwanted burden" critique — she's a psychiatric detainee
  screaming "Don't look at me" — but the prose then sides with the officers' rapture
  and inventories her body ("everything symmetrical and perfect… full dark red
  lips… her exposed face and breasts"), so the reader is positioned to admire
  alongside the men the scene means to critique. Her non-consent is overridden by the
  text treating her nudity as marvel.
- The reflex recurs: the mermaid "small breasts and sapphire nipples" (p.140); the
  King's "callipygian beauty" (p.148); the elephant-mating memory dwelling on the
  male's anatomy (p.19).
- **An important counter-note (to your credit):** by the closing threesome the gaze is
  actually *distributed* — Bernard's round belly and "erection raised unashamed,
  afraid" get the same unsparing attention as the women's bodies (pp.296–97). The
  academic reader specifically credited this: "that's not nothing." So the tool exists
  in your kit; the gaze issue is about the *default reflex* earlier, not an inability
  to do otherwise.
- **The deeper structural version (worth sitting with):** the genuine, tender lesbian
  relationship is ultimately folded into a reproductive triad, narrated start to
  finish by the man, whose redemption it services and whose three-souled child the
  women carry. As the academic put it: *"The women's love is real. It is also,
  structurally, in the end, his story."* This is the subtlest gaze note in the book
  and the one least fixable by a line edit — it's about whose story the architecture
  serves.

## 4. Instrumental use of marginalized figures (the cumulative pattern) — MODERATE

No single instance is egregious, but the *accumulation* is the note: non-white,
foreign, poor, queer, disabled, or mentally-ill figures repeatedly appear as texture,
threat, or comedy around the fully-interior white-coded leads.
- **Villain-voiced but uncounterweighted:** the "beaner" body the reality-TV god wears
  as a disposable costume (pp.322–323, with the slur played for flippancy); Asian
  women as sacrificial spectacle in the same chapter ("I love the Japanese… Sick ass
  fucks," a nameless woman's near-drowning as Bernard's moral test, pp.323–324). The
  contempt is clearly the villain's — but the narrative still *uses* these bodies as
  spectacle without a counterweight on the page.
- **Queerness as grotesque punchline:** Jerome's defining trait is a humiliation kink
  serviced by "four homosexual men in Boston" (French-maid costume, urination) — the
  only gay men in the passage exist to dispense degradation (pp.258–259). Compare how
  fully Vida/Maya are drawn: the book clearly *can* write queerness as personhood, so
  this reads as a lapse, not the book's stance.
- **Exoticized foreigners as flavor:** Billy Chen from a "one rickshaw town," ESL-as-
  comedy (p.269); the "attractive Korean woman" whose backstory is the family
  Seven-Eleven (p.105); Saki the Japanese "host" who exists only as an exotic sexual
  accessory (pp.156, 170); the Ulithian "polyamorous fishing commune" (p.224); Gandhi
  as a go-kart gag with "beady eyes and an aquiline nose" (p.80); India as
  "mystics-and-squalor" (p.85); performers tagged only by nationality (a Ukrainian
  juggler, a Jamaican juggler, a pair of Welsh jugglers).
- **Disability as novelty:** "one-armed ballerina," "blind body-builder," "Crazy
  Louie's Discount Prosthetics," and the "Midget Gynecologists" slur-gag (pp.79, 95).
- **Mental illness as flavor/romance:** schizophrenia recast as "untrained shamanism"
  (pp.209–210) and Vitoria's compulsive pill-popping as a running gag — the villain's
  menace fused with her medicated psychiatric condition.
- **Latina-domestic and pimp shorthand:** the imaginary thieving maid "Consuela"
  (p.146); the "pimp's Cadillac" carpet as gaudy-luxury shorthand (p.282).

## 5. Loaded language worth knowing you used — MODERATE/HIGH

- **"drapetomania" (p.49)** used as a whimsical vocabulary nugget for a free white
  teenager's wanderlust. The word was a 19th-century pseudo-scientific "diagnosis"
  invented to pathologize enslaved Black people who fled captivity. Deployed with no
  acknowledgment of that origin, it's the kind of thing an informed reader will stop
  on hard. Flagged not to shame the choice — it reads as a word-collector's find — but
  because you'd almost certainly want to know what it is before a reprint.
- **"gypsy itch" (p.49)** — "gypsy" is a slur for Romani people; the narration adopts
  it approvingly to romanticize wanderlust.

## 6. Appropriation of living sacred figures — MODERATE (awareness item)

Bernard is revealed as **Tonenili**, an actual Diné (Navajo) water deity, reincarnated
as a white-coded street performer; the **Zorya** (real Slavic goddesses), **Heqet**,
**Coatlicue**, and **Eshu** are woven in similarly. Borrowing living/closed Indigenous
sacred figures as free-floating reincarnation lore for non-Indigenous protagonists is
a recognized concern distinct from using Greek/Roman myth (a dead, public pantheon).
The text's "one aspect, many names" framing is doing deliberate syncretic work, so
this is flagged for awareness rather than as an error — but Tonenili and Eshu
specifically are from living traditions.

## How to hold all this

The through-line: **this is a book whose heart is generous and whose reflexes are
sometimes careless with exactly the people it most wants to celebrate.** The academic
guest reader's verdict is the fairest one-line summary — *"beautiful, generous,
occasionally careless… the prose moved me; the politics are more performed than
earned."* Almost everything here is fixable without touching what makes the book
yours, because the book already demonstrates it can do better (Vida/Maya, the
distributed gaze in the finale). The Hermes/Eshu question is the one that's genuinely
a structural decision rather than a line edit — and it's the one you already knew was
waiting for you.

*Every page reference is to the second-edition PDF. Where these overlap changes you're
already making for edition 3, treat them as confirmation, not new work.*
