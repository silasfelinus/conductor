# Guest Reviews — Mermaids of Venice

Three honest reader reviews from deliberately different vantage points, each a
full(ish) read of the second edition. They were asked to be honest, not kind — so
these aren't blurbs. Read together they trace a consistent shape: everyone admires the
prose and the invention, everyone gets snagged by the vocabulary-flexing, and each
reader's own lens finds a different real thing (Donut: the cats and the vulgarity;
the SF fan: mechanism vs. mood; the academic: whose story it is).

---

## 1. Princess Donut (of *Dungeon Crawler Carl*) — 3½ salmon treats out of 5

> ### Finally, A Book That Understands Cats Are the Only Sensible Characters

Darlings. Princess Donut has read a *book*. An entire one. Carl doubted I would finish
it — Carl doubts many things, which is why Carl needs me — but I persevered, because a
queen must occasionally lower herself into the squabbles of the smaller folk, and
because someone whispered that there were cats.

And oh. The *cats*. The very first thing this Silas Knight person gets correct is the
House of Talking Cats, where every cat crowns herself with a glorious name and a train
of honorifics — Empress of the Leftmost Bookshelf, Whose Will Keeps the Television
Ghosts from Escaping — while dismissing every lesser cat as merely "The One with the
Whiskers." These creatures keep a calendar of only four days and flatly refuse to
believe the other cats even exist. Reader, this is not fantasy. This is a
*documentary*. I felt SEEN.

The human, Betty Lou, is cursed with beauty so perfect she cannot bear to leave her
home. THIS I understand intimately. Grand-champion, best in show, the terrible weight
of being gawked at everywhere one loafs — yes. I extended her my full and gracious
sympathy.

Where the book forfeits treats: the author is exhaustingly, insufferably in love with
his own vocabulary. Every chapter is titled some tongue-cramping word no self-
respecting cat would deign to lick. Show-off. And the CRUDENESS. A junkyard described
as though it were a lady's unmentionables? Elephants engaged in unspeakable congress?
My delicate Persian constitution required three treats and a lie-down.

But credit where it is earned, and I am nothing if not fair: when the overlooked lump
Bernard wheels an injured fire-dancer straight past the police by making them both
*invisible* — because no one ever truly SEES anyone — I sat bolt upright. Sharp. And
the decayed god who now feeds on reality television and fifteen glorious seconds of
human desperation? Devastatingly accurate. Carl watches that filth. I have said so for
YEARS.

The ending, with an unborn child carrying three souls, is very pleased with itself.
Perhaps one whisker too pleased — rather like Carl after slaying one measly little
monster.

**Verdict:** A dazzling, overstuffed, occasionally vulgar little jewelry box of a book
— well worth the pawing, provided you skip the elephant. Now someone bring me a treat.
I have *earned* it.

## 2. The 20-year-old Heinlein fan (San Francisco) — ★★★ (3/5)

> ### Big ideas, drowned in fog

Okay, full disclosure: this is not my lane. I read Heinlein. I like a competent guy
solving a hard problem with his brain and a clean line from page one to the payoff.
*Mermaids of Venice* is basically the opposite of that on purpose, so take my three
stars as a guy trying to be fair to a book that was never written for him.

Here's what got me: Bernard. The eidetic-memory word nerd is the one character who
thinks like I want a character to think. There's a bit late in the book where he's
climbing a collapsing tower and he taps a wall and *counts the milliseconds for the
echo*, licks a light switch for the iron taste, clocks a fire extinguisher serial
number as a perfect square to confirm which floor he's on. That's engineering-brain
problem-solving and I ate it up. And the tower collapse itself is a real setpiece —
the building's support beams literally hold a conversation and decide to quit, and
Bernard gets replaced by fifteen tons of ceiling mid-sentence. That *goes somewhere*.
More of that and I'd have been sold.

The trouble is everything around Bernard is mood, not machinery. My idea-hungry brain
kept perking up — gods as functions, reincarnation, a universe where every being
contributes one puzzle piece, Venice sinking because it "was already dead, a memory
pretending to be a city." Cool premises! But when the cosmology finally gets
*explained*, it's a mystical download during a threesome, and the rules turn out to be
"well, guidelines at least." That's my whole beef with this kind of fantasy. Heinlein
would've given you the mechanism. Knight gives you a vibe and a wink. The book
literally opens promising you'll figure out why Venice sank and then never actually
tells you. Ambiguity as the answer. I distrust that.

And the prose. Every chapter's titled with some obscure word (Autofloccinaucinihili-
pilification, seriously) and the sentences are lush to the point of soupy. When it
lands — Betty Lou barricaded in an attic wardrobe hiding from cops she thinks are
ghosts, her cats mocking her — it's genuinely vivid. When it doesn't, you're wading.
The pacing crawls through the first act, then sprints the last fifty pages.

But — credit where due — the guy can *write*, the emotional stuff about Vida and her
dead wife actually landed on me, and the ending, Bernard choosing to become the god of
street performers narrating to an unborn kid, earned a real feeling I wasn't
expecting.

**Verdict:** not my kind of book, and I still didn't hate it — which is honestly the
best compliment I've got. I wouldn't hand it to my SF crew; they'd bounce off the
prose by page ten. But I'd hand it to my artsy roommate and tell her I think she'd
love it, and mean it.

## 3. The 32-year-old queer POC academic (last read: bell hooks) — ★★★½ (3.5/5)

> ### Gorgeous, Glutted, and a Little Guilty About Whose Story It's Telling

I wanted to resist this book and couldn't, quite. Knight writes sentences that earn
their keep — the gothic overture with Betty Lou (pp. 10–15), a woman who has padded
and painted her monstrous-beautiful body into invisibility, hiding in a wardrobe while
her own blood goes *tink, tink* onto the floor, is as good an opening as I've read in
years. The mythic register is lush without being soupy: a mermaid surfaces with
"creamy moon-skin, small breasts and sapphire nipples" (p. 140), an "electronic
windmill" gathers darkness instead of light, and the whole cosmology of dying,
reincarnating gods hums underneath the street-performer plot. The chapter-title
vocabulary flexing and the rhyming couplet epigraphs are a lot, but they're *fun* — a
writer visibly delighting in language, which I will forgive nearly anything for.

What I forgive less is who gets to be a person here. Consider Hermes, the "older black
man" with a "salt-and-pepper beard" and "crooked yellow teeth that could have bricked
the road to Oz," who calls everyone "brother," runs a pickpocket-magician hustle,
stages a fake death-by-machine, slips the hero the plot-coupon that launches the
entire novel, murmurs "tell Death Eshu says hello," and then — gone (pp. 66–69). One
appearance. The name "Eshu" is dropped exactly once. Yes, everyone in this book is
secretly a god deployed as a device; the instrumentality is structural. But Eshu is
the only one whose device comes pre-loaded with gold teeth, a con, and jive cadence.
Dignifying a Yoruba trickster and then coding him in minstrel shorthand, then
vanishing him — that's the Magical Negro with a mythology footnote. (The throwaway
"Two Wongs Make Rice" gag on p. 66 tells me the racial ear here is not careful.)

The gaze is more interesting than I expected. Knight actually distributes it — Vida's
desire for Maya carries the yoga-class meet-cute ("I Heart Your Vagina" shirt and all,
p. 62), and Bernard's round belly and "erection raised unashamed, afraid" get the same
unsparing attention as any woman's body in the closing threesome (pp. 296–97). That's
not nothing. But watch what the plot *does* with the queer couple: a genuine, funny,
tender lesbian relationship is ultimately folded into a reproductive triad, narrated
start to finish by the man, whose redemption it services and whose three-souled child
they carry. The women's love is real. It is also, structurally, in the end, *his*
story.

So: beautiful, generous, occasionally careless with exactly the people it's most eager
to celebrate. The prose moved me; the politics are more performed than earned.

**Verdict:** Read it for the language and the mermaids — just notice who gets an
interior life and who gets a cameo and a stereotype.
