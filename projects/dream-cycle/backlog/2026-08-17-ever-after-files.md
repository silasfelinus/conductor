## The idea
In a city where happy endings are bought and sold as futures above the stage, a playwright who was written out of her own hit show goes back on opening night to take her ending off the actress currently wearing it.

## Dream vibe (1)
**Third-Act Money** — Somebody is trading your ending on a floor you will never be shown.
Art: A gilded theatre seen from the flies at night, brokers hanging motionless along the rafter beams, gold thread strung between them catching the stage-light rising from below, rain on the high windows.

## Dream location (1)
- **The Gilded Rafters** — known for endings bought and sold in the beams above a working stage. Local rule: no bidding while the curtain is up. Best scene: the whole rafter goes still mid-bid because a real ending just walked in the door. Art: Dust-thick timber beams above a lit stage, brokers folded thin and motionless along them like branches, gold starfruit-fiber thread strung bead to bead, warm light leaking up through the boards.

## Character (1)
- **Ottoline Sarr** — take back the ending that was cut out of her and sold on. Carries the only surviving copy of her original third act, folded into eight. Complication: she has waited so long to be owed this that she no longer wants it back gently. Look: A tall stick-insect woman in a soaked evening coat, limbs long and dry-brown and jointed twice too often, a narrow face lit hard from below, gold thread wound and knotted along one forearm.

## Reward item (1)
- **The Understudy's Shawl** (ITEM, RARE) — makes the wearer read as whoever the room was already expecting. Best used when you need to be somewhere your face is not welcome. The catch: the room's expectation is what shows, not anything you chose. Look: A loose knitted shawl of gold starfruit fiber gone soft with handling, one corner unravelling into a long loose thread, warm lamplight caught in the weave.

## Reward skill (1)
- **Rewrite On Your Feet** (SKILL, UNCOMMON) — changes how a scene ends while it is still being played. Best used when the room has committed to a version of events. The catch: whatever it changes, it changes for everyone who was there, permanently. Look: Wet black ink blooming up through printed lines on a held page, letters sliding into new positions while the paper is still in someone's hand.
